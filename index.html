Money Sent Home

Do migrant remittances improve human development? A look across 115 developing economies, and the confounder that hides the answer.

Source: World Bank World Development Indicators

1 · The scale: remittances vs the other money flows

Money that migrants send home has quietly become one of the largest financial flows into the developing world, overtaking foreign aid decades ago and now rivaling foreign investment. Click a series in the legend to isolate it.

2 · Where it matters most

For the most dependent economies, remittances are not a side flow, but a major share of the entire economy. Top ten in 2022.

3 · The twist: a relationship hidden by income

Do countries that receive more remittances have better child survival? At first glance, barely. But remittances flow to poorer countries, and poverty drives up child mortality on its own. Remove income from both sides, and the real relationship appears. Toggle "Adjust for income" to watch it happen.

4 · Prediction: the odds of meeting a global goal

A logistic model (holding national income constant) turns the finding into a prediction. The UN's child-survival target is 25 or fewer under-five deaths per 1,000. Move the slider to set a country's remittance level and see its predicted odds of missing that target.

5 · Country spotlight: a lever for policymakers

Pick a country to see its remittance inflows, whether it meets the UN child-survival target, and one concrete policy lever, the cost of sending money home. Sending still averages about 6% globally; the UN target (SDG 10.c) is 3%, and every point saved stays with families. Move the cost slider to size the prize.

How to read this. Each point is a country in 2022. "Adjust for income" shows a partial-regression view: the part of remittances and of child mortality that is not explained by GDP per capita, which is why those axes are centred on zero. The prediction model controls for national income.

Caveats. This is observational data, so it shows association, not proof of causation, and works at the country level rather than the household level.
